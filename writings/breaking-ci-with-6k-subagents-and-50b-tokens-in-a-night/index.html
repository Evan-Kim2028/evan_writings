Breaking CI with 6k Subagents and 50B Tokens in a Night

6,000 agent sessions and 50B tokens in one night jammed CI with 150 half-finished PRs. Local test gates and merge trains cut test time per PR five-fold.

Evan Kim agents ci devops merge-trains muse space-bunny deepseek

Intro

A couple weeks ago, I took advantage of the free unnamed model space bunny. I quickly discovered that there were truly "no limits" by spinning up about 100 subagents at once (over 4,000 output tokens/second combined at peak) in an attempt to work through my GitHub issue backlog.

I had one open question: "If I burn tens of billions of tokens, what does the output look like?"

Excited, I started a workflow and went to sleep. The next day, almost 50 billion tokens later, I woke up eager to see what progress had been made. To my chagrin, a massive traffic jam had occurred somewhere. There were about 150 open PRs across both repos, all half finished, and the agent swarm had stopped working sometime during the night.

An initial diagnosis revealed that the bottleneck was CI. It couldn't support the sustained burst of work from hundreds of agents. By design, there was no hosted CI in the loop, to avoid spending money on hosted runners. Each agent would run tests on its own branch locally and merge on green. This was fine at sub-hundred-agent scale, but not at a scale of thousands of agents. Nearly 6,000 agents had launched, but only 156 of them got as far as opening a PR and only 3 of those PRs merged.

Digging further, the problems became apparent:

  1. The test selector picked far too much. A 100-line change often ran the full suite, so even simple PRs jammed up the CI.
  2. Every PR was also being tested about twice, both times on the runners: once when the branch was pushed, and again on main after it merged, plus re-runs of failed or superseded jobs.

I diverted the fleet to cut down every inefficiency I saw. The goal was simple. If I change a few files, the PR should merge with green CI within a minute or two.

Fixing both of these issues cut test time per PR about five-fold. The average PR wait went from ~60 minutes to under 5, which let me triple output from ~60 to ~180 PRs a day while spending fewer resources on CI.

Fixing the CI

On Oct 2, the full suite was thinned out and stopped from running on every PR, but it made no real difference. The real impact came on Oct 5, when CI runs per PR were cut from 2 to 1.

There was a brief spike on Oct 9, when a massive database migration workflow, roughly +50k/-50k LOC, introduced a bug in the CI. The fix landed that night and minutes per PR dropped back down.

Today the CI is holding up great. Over the last ~10 days and 30 billion tokens, there have been no more traffic jams, and Oct 10 was the first time breaking over the 200 PR count.

Local test gates and merge trains

Now that CI was under control, the next step was future-proofing the codebase. If the number of PRs doubles, how does the time to ship a PR stay the same, or better yet drop? Only a quarter of runner test time went to the run that let a PR merge. The rest was failure re-runs, runs cancelled by a newer push, and the re-test on main after every merge.

Two key observations.

  1. Agent fleet work is bursty. Over 24 hours there is plenty of compute available on the VPS and laptops, but most of the work comes while the human is managing the fleet during daylight hours across multiple terminals.
  2. It was clear that the amount of re-computations from re-runs and run cancellations needed to be crushed.

To handle the bursts, the bulk of the CI and running tests was moved locally to the laptops. Initially self-hosted runners were run on a VPS server to get away from cloud hosted solutions. However that became clogged within the day due to the agent PR bursts. Since the laptop generally had spare capacity and more compute available, the bursts could be more easily absorbed and managed.

To reduce the amount of computational redundancy, PR trains were adopted. The main orchestrator agent batches related branches into merge trains where a single monolithic PR can carry anywhere from 3 to over 20 branches of work. The end result was that merging would not trigger a merging storm, and since all of the CI was run locally on every branch, there was no need to re-run most of the CI again after merge. Trains have been very productive so far. Over the last six days, 109 trains merged next to about 390 single-branch PRs.
